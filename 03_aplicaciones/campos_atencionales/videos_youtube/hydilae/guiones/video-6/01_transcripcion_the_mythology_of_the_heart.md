# The Mythology of the Heart — Frank Nager

## I. The essence of symbols

> He who reconciles many contradictions  
> in his life, and gratefully  
> subsumes them under one symbol,  
> expels the noisy crowd from  
> his abode.  
> (Rainer Maria Rilke: *Book of Hours*)

The study of the history of culture – from stone-age cave paintings to the ideals and idols of our own era – shows us that human beings need symbols. Symbols are complementary to the rational world of concepts, and they allow us to see that many of the things around us, apart from their measurability and usefulness, have another and deeper meaning. The practice of our mathematical and technical age to look back to ancient cultures and religions, its remarkable interest in myths and fairy tales, its inclination towards the mystical, the esoteric and meditation are clearly necessary and compensatory countercurrents against the imperious pressure of our overweening technologies to ‘measure what can be measured’ (Galilei), to make use of what can be made use of, to subjugate what can be subjugated.

An openness to symbols and serious consideration of them directs human beings into themselves and helps them to find themselves and to give their lives greater meaning and integrity amid the noisy bustle of a hectic era. As the opening quotation from Rainer Maria Rilke states, symbols can reconcile the many contradictions in life and drive the all too troublesome tendencies from the ‘abode’, that is, the heart.

The word *symbol* (Greek *symbolon*) comes from the verb *symballein* (‘put together’): an inner connection is made between symbol and what it represents.

Erich Fromm distinguishes between conventional or accidental symbols and universal symbols. Conventional symbols (such as initial letters, say CH, for *Confoederatio Helvetica*, to represent Switzerland) have a purely informative function. Universal symbols, also called primal symbols, have a significative character and convey a deeper meaning. Universal symbols, for example fire, water, the cross, the mandala, the heart are – by contrast with the conventional type – not rationally invented but archetypal. They arise from the primal roots of collective human experience.

According to M. Lurker, primal symbols form the ‘deepest layer of every conception of the world; through them a material and sensually apprehensible reality is referred to a numinous transcendent power’.

Symbols may be considered signposts to the unresearchable (Kessler). Not being founded in reason, their depths cannot be plumbed. Instead, symbolic thinking requires intuition and imagination. It is a form of thinking in analogies and syntheses, a *raison du cœur* which aspires to the whole. Symbolic thinking is alert to deeper connections. It attempts to seek the foundations of ‘the eternal, the essence’, that unresearchable yet experienceable primal basis ‘which holds the inmost part of the world together’:

> Making out such picture-writing  
> Life will recompense full well,  
> For the essence, the eternal,  
> Surely in myself do dwell.  
> (Hermann Hesse: *Confession*)
