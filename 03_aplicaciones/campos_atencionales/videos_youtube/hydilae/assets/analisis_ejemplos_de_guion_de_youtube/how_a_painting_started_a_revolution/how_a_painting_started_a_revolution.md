The Philippine Revolution was inspired by a painting. After 300 years under the Spanish rule, the Philippines declared independence on June 12th, 1898. The fight against colonialism was strengthened by the Propagandists, a reformist movement aiming at legally affirming equality between Spanish and Filipinos, protecting civil rights, and give more power to the Philippines inside the Spanish Empire, notably by giving Filipinos Spanish citizenship, making the Philippines a Spanish province, and granting Filipinos representation in the Spanish parliament.

Two popular propagandists were Jose Rizal and Graciano Lopez Jaena. Both were in Madrid in 1884, and this was a great place to be if you were Filipino. Two Filipino artists had won prestigious prizes at the National Exhibition of Fine Arts in Madrid. Felix Hidalgo won a silver medal with his Christian Virgins Exposed to the Populace, but even more impressively, Juan Luna won the first gold medal with his Spoliarium.

An Asian subject of a colonized territory bested every Spanish artist in the competition. That was incredible news, especially for the Filipino nationalists who were looking for evidence against the claim that they were inferior.

After Luna won the gold medal, a celebration was organized in a café and speeches were made. Rizal would say that the painting represents the spirit of our social, moral, and spiritual life, humanity subjected to trials unredeemed, reason in open fight with prejudice, fanaticism, and injustice. However, I prefer Lopez Jaena's speech. The Philippines is more than a veritable Spoliarium with all its horrors. There lies in mangled fragments, humanity massacred, the rights of man perverted. There is no semblance of justice, for the common man and liberty is cinders, ashes, and dust.

This according to China. This, according to John Silva, senior consultant at the National Museum of the Philippines, is what inspired revolution, or at least reformation in these thinkers. So let's look at Juan Luna's Spoliarium. It's an enormous painting, measuring 7.7 meters by 4.2 meters. It's a dark image, both in its tonal values and its subject matter. It depicts a spoliarium, the basement of a Roman amphitheater where the corpses of dead gladiators would be thrown.

From there, they'd be stripped from their armor and weapons. A lot is going on in this painting. First and most importantly, dead bodies are being dragged in the middle of the composition. You can tell Luna wants us to look right in the center of this painting. This is where the scene is most lit. It's where vibrant red can be seen, and it's where the composition is the most dynamic, through horizontal lines and a centered diamond. However, once you're initially shocked by the dead gladiators, your eye starts to wander. On the left, you see scavengers looking at their possible loot. A man is pushing another one away. As you travel more to the left, you see a crowd going upwards through a staircase, indicating that the Spoliarium is underground.

On the right, however, the scene becomes even more grim. A woman is mourning the death of her husband, or maybe her son. A father is looking for his son amongst the pile of dead bodies. This painting is one of death and suffering, not only the suffering of the gladiators, dead or injured, but the suffering of the ones they loved, their wives or parents. And something important to note as well is that all this death and suffering is senseless, it's avoidable. Perhaps Luna was equating Roman cruelty with Spanish cruelty, equating Rome's violent imperialism with Spain's violent colonialism. No matter what his intentions were, the social commentary behind this painting was inevitable. We're talking about a gigantic, important painting, one which, through its sheer size, makes a statement.

The very fact that the statement of an artwork was to win the first gold medal would, of course, have an impact. But the most important reason as to why this painting was to become a social commentary was because its artist was from one of Spain's colonies. His identity as a colonized person was going to contribute to the interpretation of this painting. Even if Luna was in favor of Spanish colonialism, this painting would still, by virtue of being presented by a Filipino artist, be an anti-colonial artwork. It presents a behind-the-scenes image of the cruelty and bloodshed of an empire, and it's portrayed by a colonial subject, the talent and genius of which not even his colonial masters could deny.
