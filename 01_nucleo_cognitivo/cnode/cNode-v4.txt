TITLE: Cognitive Node (cNode / Nodo Cognitivo) — Projection-Centered Ontology Package (Weak ↔ Strong)
VERSION: 4.1.0
STATUS: Working Draft (exportable; minimal ambiguity target)
LANGUAGE: English (chosen for precision and review stability)
AUDIENCE: Any project with zero prior context about Cognitive Nodes
SCOPE: Definition + construction method + integration rules for cNodes as ontologies in a graph of cNodes
NON-SCOPE: Neuroscience claims; therapy; user interface (UI) design; deployment; any single storage technology

================================================================================
1) PURPOSE OF THIS DOCUMENT (EBDC: Estructura Base de Definición Conceptual)
================================================================================
Define “cNode (Cognitive Node)” as a self-contained, repeatable, integrable ontology package that
captures a cognitive structure by projection, so it can be shared across contexts without hidden
personal assumptions. This document specifies what must be preserved (invariants), what may change
(domain of variation), and how cNodes connect and compose into larger graphs.

================================================================================
2) PRIMARY OBJECTIVE
================================================================================
Enable humans and systems to externalize, validate, reuse, and integrate cognitive structures as
explicit ontologies, ranging from weak (minimal vocabulary) to strong (formal constraints), without
losing identity across adaptations.

================================================================================
3) CORE DEFINITION (STRICT)
================================================================================
A cNode (Cognitive Node) is:
(1) an ontology (weak ↔ strong),
(2) produced by a deliberate projection of an internal cognitive structure (mNode),
(3) packaged as an explicit, self-contained unit,
(4) designed to integrate into a graph of other cNodes through declared interfaces and relations.

In short: cNode = ontology-as-a-portable-projection of a cognitive structure.

================================================================================
4) KEY TERMS (NO ASSUMPTIONS)
================================================================================
Ontology:
  An explicit specification of entities (concepts), relations (edges), and constraints that define
  an identity boundary for a domain. “Weak” ontologies may be only a vocabulary + relations; “strong”
  ontologies add types, rules, invariants, validation, and integration policies.

Graph:
  A network of nodes and edges. Here: nodes are cNodes (each with its own ontology). Edges are typed
  relations declared by the cNodes, not accidental associations.

Edge (Relation):
  A declared, interpretable connection between two nodes or entities, with a meaning and constraints.
  Edges have directionality when needed (source → target), plus a rationale and validation.

mNode (Mental Node / Internal Cognitive Structure):
  An internal, identity-carrying cognitive form in a human mind that guides perception, reasoning,
  decisions, learning, or execution.

Cognitive structure families (source-forms for mNodes):
  - idea: “what this is”
  - plan: “what steps to follow”
  - method: “how to produce outcomes reliably”
  - design: “how parts relate to satisfy constraints”
  - reasoning pattern: “how conclusions are derived”
  Extensible families: schema, mental model, heuristic, skill routine, protocol, evaluation frame,
  control policy, classification rule, decision policy.

Projection (Operation):
  The deliberate act of selecting and externalizing from an mNode:
  - invariants (what must be preserved),
  - domain of variation (what may change),
  - entities and relations (what exists and how it relates),
  - procedures (how it is used to produce outcomes),
  - validation criteria (what counts as correct/compatible),
  while excluding accidental personal exemplars, private context, and irrelevant associations.

Meta-metaphor:
  A metaphor that functions as a standardized structural bridge (not as decoration) for moving an
  internal cognitive structure into a shared external ontology package. In this project, “cNode” can
  be treated as the meta-metaphor that enforces projection-readiness and integration-readiness.

================================================================================
5) CORE INVARIANT (IDENTITY BOUNDARY)
================================================================================
Not every ontology is a cNode. A cNode is an ontology that satisfies the projection condition:

  Projection condition:
  The ontology is produced by projecting an mNode (or by projecting a composite mNode formed from
  other cNodes). Even if a cNode is built exclusively from other cNodes, the act of construction is
  still a projection operation performed by a human (or an explicit process) that selects boundaries.

This condition is a defining invariant: “cNode-ness” is tied to origin by projection, not only to
having a vocabulary.

================================================================================
6) INVARIANTS (WHAT MUST NOT BREAK)
================================================================================
I1) Self-contained meaning:
    A cNode must be usable without hidden personal context. All critical terms are defined or linked
    to internal definitions within the cNode.

I2) Explicit identity boundary:
    The cNode states invariants and domain of variation. Identity is not “the words”, but the
    preserved structure across adaptations.

I3) Relational explicitness:
    Entities and relations are explicit. “Implicit” relations must be promoted to explicit edges or
    excluded as accidental.

I4) Projection traceability:
    The cNode records the projection rationale: what was selected, what was excluded, and why.

I5) Integrability:
    The cNode declares how it can connect to other cNodes (interfaces), and how conflicts are handled
    during integration (merge policy).

I6) Deterministic interpretation:
    Given the same cNode, independent readers can derive the same operational meaning within the
    stated domain of variation.

I7) Compositionality:
    A cNode may contain internal nodes that are:
    - concepts (entities in its ontology), and/or
    - other cNodes (sub-ontologies),
    with explicit relations between them.

I8) Entity alignment (mNode → cNode):
    Entities inside an mNode can exist implicitly (as unnamed parts of thought). Projection converts
    them into explicit ontology entities. The cNode entities are the externalized counterparts of the
    mNode’s implicit structure, not separate unrelated “things”.

================================================================================
7) DOMAIN OF VARIATION (WHAT MAY CHANGE)
================================================================================
V1) Level of formality:
    A cNode may be weak (minimal vocabulary) or strong (formal types + rules). Strength can evolve.

V2) Contextual realizations:
    The same cNode may be adapted to different environments, cultures, or projects, as long as the
    invariants remain intact.

V3) Representation format:
    The cNode can be stored as text, diagrams, JavaScript Object Notation (JSON), JavaScript Object
    Notation Lines (JSONL), or YAML (YAML Ain't Markup Language, a human-readable data format), or
    any medium that preserves the explicit structure.

V4) Integration mechanism:
    Integration can be done via “graph patches” or other methods. Graph patch is important, but it is
    not the center of the concept; it is one possible integration artifact.

================================================================================
8) INTERVENTION (THE PROJECTION ACT AS A TRANSFORMATION)
================================================================================
The projection from mNode → cNode can be modeled analogically as a linear transformation in linear
algebra:

- mNode space (domain):
  The space of internal cognitive structures (ideas, plans, methods, designs, reasoning patterns).

- cNode space (codomain):
  The space of explicit ontologies (portable structures).

- Projection operator:
  The act that maps an mNode into a cNode by selecting a basis of description (core terms, relations,
  invariants) and discarding irrelevant components.

Important nuance:
  The goal is not “what a project requires”, but “the best projection that preserves identity while
  enabling integration and reuse”. Requirements can constrain the projection, but do not define it.

================================================================================
9) MANIFESTATIONS (OUTPUTS OF A cNode)
================================================================================
A cNode can manifest as:
- a reusable definition package (repeatable, explainable, auditable),
- an executable procedure (protocol) derived from the ontology,
- a graph segment integrated into a larger cognitive graph,
- a family of contextual realizations that preserve identity.

================================================================================
10) MINIMAL STRUCTURE (COMPONENTS)
================================================================================
A minimal cNode must contain:

C1) Context block:
    - what it is,
    - what it is for,
    - where it applies (scope),
    - what it excludes (non-scope),
    - assumptions explicitly declared (never implicit).

C2) Vocabulary (entities / concepts):
    - defined terms,
    - types (optional in weak regime; required in strong regime),
    - examples for each term (simple, minimal).

C3) Relations (edges):
    - relation names,
    - direction and semantics,
    - constraints (optional in weak regime; required in strong regime),
    - examples of valid and invalid relations.

C4) Invariants + domain of variation:
    - identity boundary,
    - permitted adaptations.

C5) Procedures (optional but recommended):
    - how to use the cNode to produce outcomes,
    - how to compose it with other cNodes.

C6) Validation:
    - checks that detect violations of invariants,
    - compatibility checks for integration.

C7) Integration surface:
    - what the cNode provides,
    - what it requires,
    - merge policy for conflicts.

Construction order note:
  There is no single “correct” starting point. Any component can be drafted first. A valid cNode is
  one that satisfies the invariants and yields deterministic interpretation after completion.

================================================================================
11) REPRESENTATION LAYERS (ONTOLOGY + OPTIONAL INTEGRATION ARTIFACTS)
================================================================================
Layer A: cNode-as-ontology (core)
  The ontology itself: entities, relations, invariants, procedures, validation, interfaces.

Layer B: Integration artifacts (optional)
  Example: graph_patch (Graph Patch) = an instruction set that inserts/links the cNode into a target
  graph. It may include node identifiers, edge inserts, alias mappings, and conflict resolution steps.
  Graph patch is important when integration must be automated or replayable.

Clarification:
  The cNode does not reduce to {cognitive_node, graph_patch}. Those are useful representations, not
  the whole concept. The concept is the ontology produced by projection, plus integrability.

================================================================================
12) COMPOSITION INTO A NETWORK OF cNodes (ALWAYS-INTEGRATABLE PRINCIPLE)
================================================================================
Goal:
  Any cNode can integrate with any other cNode, provided explicit interfaces exist or can be mapped.

Minimum integration interface (export surface):
- PROVIDES: capabilities, concepts, relations, procedures
- REQUIRES: dependencies (concepts or capabilities needed from others)
- CONSTRAINTS: invariants that must hold after integration
- TYPES: canonical types used (or type aliases)
- ALIASES: mapping for edge names and type names

Integration profile (negotiation procedure):
- discover → check compatibility → map aliases → validate → adapt → merge → report

Merge policy (determinism requirement):
- conflict handling rules,
- identifier collision policy,
- provenance (trace of what was merged and why).

================================================================================
13) METAPHOR AND META-METAPHOR IN THE cNode CONCEPT
================================================================================
Metaphor (functional use):
  A metaphor is used to transfer structure, not words. Examples: “port”, “mold”, “scaffold”.
  A functional metaphor must be tied to explicit invariants and relations, not to imagery alone.

Meta-metaphor (standardizing use):
  A meta-metaphor is the stable pattern that makes metaphors operational and integrable. In this
  project, “cNode” is treated as a meta-metaphor: it is the standardized container that forces the
  projection of an mNode into an explicit ontology with integration rules.

================================================================================
14) EXAMPLES (STRUCTURAL, NOT ONLY LINGUISTIC)
================================================================================
Example A: “Dog” vs personal exemplars
- Two humans are asked to think of “a dog”.
  Human 1 imagines a Dalmatian from childhood.
  Human 2 imagines a German Shepherd owned today.
- Those are different manifestations (instances) guided by different personal context.
- The shared cNode “DOG” is the ontology that defines what makes a dog a dog:
  invariants (mammal, domesticated canid, typical traits), relations (has-body-part, can-be-trained),
  and a domain of variation (breed, color, size, individual history).
- “Isomorphism” nuance:
  Dalmatian and German Shepherd are not isomorphic as whole biological systems, but they can be
  treated as structurally equivalent relative to a chosen abstraction: both satisfy the DOG cNode’s
  invariants. Under that abstraction, there exists a structure-preserving mapping from each exemplar
  to the shared ontology (membership via invariant satisfaction).

Example B: Protocol cNode (fire protocol)
- Invariants: detect fire, alert, evacuate, contain, verify safety.
- Domain of variation: building layout, local regulation, available equipment.
- Relations: alarm-triggers-evacuation, extinguisher-applies-to-fire-class.
- This cNode can integrate with a building-management cNode via declared interfaces.

================================================================================
15) CONSTRUCTION PROCEDURE (HOW TO BUILD A cNode)
================================================================================
Step 1: Identify the source mNode (cognitive structure) and its family (idea, plan, method, etc.).
Step 2: Choose the projection boundary:
        - what must be preserved (invariants),
        - what may vary (domain of variation),
        - what is excluded (private exemplars, irrelevant associations).
Step 3: Define vocabulary (entities) with minimal examples.
Step 4: Define relations (edges) with semantics and constraints.
Step 5: Write invariants and variation explicitly.
Step 6: Add procedures (how to use) and validation (how to test).
Step 7: Declare integration surface (provides/requires/constraints/types/aliases) and merge policy.
Step 8: Produce at least one contextual realization to verify invariants survive adaptation.
Step 9: Integrate into a graph (optionally via graph_patch) and record provenance.

================================================================================
16) VALIDATION CHECKLIST (MINIMAL)
================================================================================
- No implicit assumptions: every critical term is defined or referenced internally.
- Identity boundary is explicit: invariants + domain of variation are written.
- Relations are explicit and testable (examples of valid/invalid edges exist).
- Projection trace is present (what was selected/excluded and why).
- Integration surface exists (provides/requires + merge policy).
- The cNode can be read and applied by an independent reader with consistent results.

END OF DEFINITION
